# Singapore: a small country turned global hub

*EC 391 Country Team Project, Fall 2026*

Singapore has almost no natural resources, no farmland to speak of and a domestic market of six million people. Yet in 2024 it produced $573 billion of output, the world's second highest income per person at purchasing power parity, and a volume of trade more than three times its GDP. This site asks how a city state with so little to start with became one of the busiest hubs of world trade and investment, and what that position implies for firms deciding whether to invest there.

Our answer, built up chapter by chapter from data we downloaded and processed ourselves, is that Singapore turned the things it does have into sources of comparative advantage. Its location on the Strait of Malacca made it a natural entrepôt, and its institutions, which rank first in the world for government effectiveness and regulatory quality, made it the place where multinational firms chose to put their factories, their regional headquarters and their money. Open trade policy and a network of 29 free trade agreements kept the door open on both sides. The result is an economy that sits in the middle of global value chains, importing a large share of the value it exports and earning its living increasingly from services such as transport and finance.

Throughout, Singapore is compared with Malaysia, Hong Kong SAR and Vietnam, and with the World Bank's East Asia and Pacific region. Malaysia is the neighbour with a similar colonial history and a larger resource base, Hong Kong SAR is the other Asian city hub, and Vietnam is the fast growing newcomer competing for the same manufacturing investment. Every indicator is given with its global and regional rank, and every chart is drawn by the team from the raw files listed in the [appendix](appendix.html). The years covered are 2004 to 2024 unless a source ends earlier.
