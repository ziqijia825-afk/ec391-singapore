# Conclusion

## What we found

Singapore began with a strategic location and almost nothing else. Over the past two decades it has turned that starting point into one of the highest incomes in the world, with GDP per person at purchasing power parity second only to one other economy. The chapters of this site trace how. Institutions that rank first in the world for government effectiveness and regulatory quality, the best logistics of any country and almost no corruption (Chapter 2) made Singapore the preferred Asian base for multinational firms. Those firms brought the capital that gives Singapore the 14th highest capital stock per worker in the world, and the country's exports moved toward the capital and skill intensive goods and services that Heckscher Ohlin predicts for such an endowment (Chapter 4). Trade is now more than three times GDP, more than half of merchandise exports are re-exports, and services have risen to about half of exports (Chapter 3). Almost half the value of Singapore's exports is imported, which places it downstream in global value chains as a processor, coordinator and distributor for the region (Chapter 5). Foreign direct investment, much of it passing through holding companies, is equal to four times GDP (Chapter 6), and a policy of zero tariffs combined with 29 free trade agreements keeps goods and capital moving (Chapter 7). The case of chips and pharmaceuticals shows that investment-led industrial policy can create a lasting advantage when it builds on an existing cluster, but that it does not guarantee one (Chapter 9).

## Investment recommendation

For a multinational firm deciding where to locate in Southeast Asia, our analysis supports Singapore as the location for activities that depend on reliability and connectivity more than on cost. These include regional headquarters, treasury and trading functions, logistics and distribution hubs, research and development, and the most capital and skill intensive stages of manufacturing, such as advanced wafer fabrication, testing of complex chips and high value pharmaceutical ingredients. For these activities Singapore's institutions, skills, infrastructure and agreements outweigh its high costs, and the Refundable Investment Credit offsets part of the effect of the global minimum tax.

For labour or land intensive production, assembly of consumer electronics and price sensitive manufacturing, Singapore is not the right choice. Malaysia, Vietnam and the Johor side of the new special economic zone offer the same regional market access at much lower cost, and a common strategy is to combine a Singapore headquarters with plants in those countries, as the outward FDI data in Chapter 6 show many firms already do. The main risk to monitor is US trade policy toward Singapore's chip and pharmaceutical exports, which could change the calculation for export platform investment aimed at the American market.
